\textbf{PROJECT SUMMARY}

About 1 in 2,000 US newborns has single-ventricle heart disease, traditionally palliated with the Fontan circulation, which routes venous blood directly to the lungs but leads many patients to heart failure and multi-organ disease. Children with a borderline ventricle, underdeveloped yet with the potential to serve as a working ventricle, can instead undergo complex biventricular repair, which divides the heart with a patch so that each ventricle pumps to its own circulation. If the ventricle is too small, surgeons first perform ventricular recruitment, redirecting more blood through the chamber so the added load grows it over months. Up to a third of Fontan palliations could be avoided, but both procedures carry risk: the repaired ventricle may fail to sustain the circulation, and a recruited ventricle may not grow enough within 6 to 15 months, returning the child to palliation after added surgery. Today's heuristic cut-offs of volume and pressure measure size but not function.

The overarching goal is to reduce the number of children who require Fontan palliation. Instead of seeking cut-offs for individual measurements, which measurement error and patient heterogeneity make unreliable, the project synthesizes magnetic resonance imaging and simultaneous catheter pressure recordings into patient-specific digital twins, biomechanical models of each child's heart and circulation. Each digital twin interprets the data consistently with biophysical principles and can be created during the exam itself. The observational study includes about 25 children per year with a borderline left or right ventricle and compares every prediction with the child's actual course without influencing care. Aim 1 quantifies each patient's physiology before surgery and validates that functional metrics, such as elastance, myocardial stiffness, and contractility, correlate better with successful repair than volume and pressure. Aim 2 simulates the planned repair in each digital twin, varying contractility, heart rate, preload, and valve regurgitation, to identify children at risk of unfavorable physiology after surgery. Aim 3 applies a mechanistic growth model to forecast from the pre-recruitment digital twin whether a recruited ventricle will grow enough for repair, validated at follow-up.

The project unites two firsts: physiology assessment by a digital twin created during the clinical exam, and a mechanistic forecast of ventricular growth after recruitment. If successful, it will replace heuristic cut-offs with validated, physiology-based criteria for selecting and timing biventricular repair, so that children who can sustain two ventricles avoid the Fontan circulation and children who cannot are spared futile operations. Uniting clinical and biomechanical teams and running on routine clinical data, the approach is built for translation: the validated criteria will anchor a future multi-center trial and open the way for digital twins in pediatric surgical decisions.
