\subsubsection{Predicting Long-Term Ventricular Growth with a Constrained Mixture Model}

Whether a recruited ventricle will grow enough to sustain a biventricular circulation is a question of long-term \gls*{gr}, and no clinical measurement forecasts it: indexed end-diastolic volume and end-diastolic pressure describe the ventricle on the day of the exam, not its trajectory.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Paradigm shift: growth as an emergent outcome, not a prescribed one.} Kinematic growth models, the field's standard for three decades, impose the direction and extent of growth by hand and thus cannot decide whether growth will be \emph{sufficient}. In the constrained mixture theory, growth emerges from myocyte and collagen turnover chasing a homeostatic mechanical set-point.

\item \textbf{First homogenized constrained mixture model of cardiac \gls*{gr}.} Developed and validated by our group, it reproduces both stable growth that returns to homeostasis and unstable growth that runs away from a single set of equations (Figure~\ref{fig:aim3prelim}); our removal of the memory barrier of classical constrained mixture models makes organ-scale growth over months of physiological time tractable.

\item \textbf{First mechanistic forecast of recruitment adequacy.} Initialized from the pre-recruitment \gls*{dt} of Aim~1 and driven by the post-recruitment loading, the model predicts chamber volume, wall thickness, and mass over the 8--15 month recruitment interval, the long-term counterpart to the short-term physiology of Aims~1 and~2.

\item \textbf{A prediction of insufficient growth is the actionable one.} It identifies, before any surgery, the children for whom recruitment would add risk without benefit, moving patient selection beyond size-and-pressure cutoffs toward a mechanistic forecast of the individual ventricle.
\end{itemize}
